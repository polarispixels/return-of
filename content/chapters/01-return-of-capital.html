Protect the money before you try to grow it. Every other rule in this method rests on that one, because growth only happens to money you still have.

Losses cost more than gains give back

Most people think about returns as a score: up 10% here, down 10% there, and the two roughly cancel. They do not. A loss shrinks the base that the next gain works on, so the climb back is always steeper than the fall.

The formula is short. If you lose a fraction L of your money, the gain you need to get back to even is L divided by (1 minus L). The table shows what that looks like.

The curve is gentle at first and then turns vertical. A 10% loss is an annoyance. A 50% loss needs a double. A 90% loss needs a tenfold gain, which is rarely a realistic plan. This is why avoiding large losses matters more than chasing large gains: the large loss is the one you may never repair.

Compounding needs survivors

Compounding is gains earning their own gains over time. It is the quiet engine behind long-term investing, and it has one requirement: the capital has to still be there. A deep loss does more than cut your balance. It resets the clock on everything that balance would have earned.

Think of it as two jobs. The first job is staying in the game. The second job is growing while you stay. Most investors who fail do so at the first job, usually by taking one position, one bet, or one bad stretch too large to survive.

Compounding only works on capital that survives.

What “return of” looks like in practice

A principle you cannot act on is a slogan. Return of capital becomes real through a short list of habits, each of which the later chapters turn into written rules.

  • Position limits. No single holding can hurt you badly. You decide the maximum size before you buy, not after the price moves.
  • Stops. Every active trade has an exit price written down before entry. The stop caps what one mistake can cost. Chapter 10 covers sizing so that hitting a stop costs a small, fixed share of the satellite.
  • Cash held for known needs. Money you will need soon stays out of the market. A forced sale at a bad price turns a temporary dip into a permanent loss. Chapter 2 sorts every dollar by when it may be needed.
  • A passive default. When you are not paying attention, the money sits in broad, low-cost funds instead of in positions that need watching.

Safety first does not mean no risk

Return of capital is not a promise to avoid all losses. Every investment carries risk, and a portfolio with no risk has no growth. The principle is about the size and shape of the losses you accept. Small, planned, survivable losses are the cost of doing business. Large, surprising, unrecoverable ones are what the rules exist to prevent.

It also sets an order of questions. Before asking how much a position could make, ask how much it could lose, and whether you could live with that number. If the answer is no, the position is too big or the idea is not ready.

This is a personal method, not advice. The thresholds that fit your situation are yours to set, and nothing here predicts what any investment will return.

Open questions

  • What is the largest loss on a single position you will accept, as a share of the satellite and of the whole portfolio?
  • What is the maximum drawdown for the whole portfolio that sends you back to the core and a pause?
  • Which stop styles (fixed percentage, chart level, trailing) will you allow, and which will you ban?
  • How will the journal show whether you honored a stop or moved it?
  • How much cash counts as “enough” for known needs, and who gets to change that number?

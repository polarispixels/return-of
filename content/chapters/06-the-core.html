The core does most of the compounding and needs almost no attention, which is exactly why it is the default. Everything else in the book sits on top of it.

Broad index funds, bought on a schedule

An index fund holds a whole market or a large slice of one, instead of a few chosen companies. A broad index ETF can hold hundreds or thousands of stocks in one purchase. You do not have to pick winners. You own the market and accept what it gives you.

This is Strategy 1, the Index Core: buy broad ETFs on a schedule and hold. The approach follows the low-cost index fund idea Bogle made famous. The schedule matters as much as the fund. Set an automatic deposit each month and let it buy without your input. The deposit does not wait for a good mood or a good headline. It is also the first automation in the Ladder, set up in Level 1.

Why does cost matter so much? A fund’s expense ratio is taken from your assets every year, whether the fund gains or loses. You cannot control the market, but you can control what you pay. Over decades, a small yearly fee takes a large share of the growth, because you lose the fee and everything that fee would have earned. When two funds hold nearly the same thing, buy the cheaper one.

Pies: your own weighted basket

A pie is a basket of funds or stocks, each with a target weight. It is Strategy 2, the Custom Pie, in the style of M1. You might split a pie among a U.S. stock index, an international stock index, and a bond index. The weights are your choice, written down once.

New deposits buy whatever is furthest below its target, and the platform can rebalance the rest for you. Risk is low to medium, depending on what you put in it. Effort stays low, because the pie runs itself.

Dividend growth and reinvestment

Strategy 3, Dividend Growth, means holding companies that keep raising their payouts, and reinvesting what they pay. A dividend is a share of profit paid to owners, usually each quarter. A company that raises its dividend year after year is showing something about the health of its business.

Reinvesting turns each payout into more shares, and those shares pay their own dividends. That loop is compounding in its plainest form. Many brokers can reinvest automatically, so no attention is needed.

Be honest about the limits. A dividend is not free money: the share price typically drops by about the amount paid out. A high yield can also signal a business in trouble. A company can cut its dividend. Look at the growth of the payout and the strength of the business behind it, not the size of the yield alone. Dividends are also taxed, which Chapter 14 covers.

Rebalancing

Over time, the winners in your core grow larger than their targets. Rebalancing means trimming what has grown and adding to what has lagged, so the mix returns to your plan. It keeps your risk where you set it.

Choose a rule and write it down. You can rebalance on a calendar date, or when a holding drifts a set distance from its target. The easiest method is to steer new deposits toward the laggards, which avoids selling and any tax from selling.

The core does most of the compounding and needs almost no attention.

Why the core is the default

The guiding rule of the book is that active management depends on activity. When the morning routine lapses, the active slice goes back to the core. The core never needs the routine, so it is the place built to be left alone. Return of capital comes first, and a low-cost, diversified, automated core is the plainest way to put that principle to work.

A good method fails if you will not follow it. Disposition is the temperament to act when the rules say act, and to do nothing when they say wait.

The rules only work if you keep them

Every chapter so far has produced a rule: size to 1%, honor the stop, stay inside the trade cap. A rule that is easy to follow when the market is quiet gets tested when it is not. Your position is down, the stop is close, and every instinct says to give it more room. That moment is where the method either holds or does not.

Write the rules down while you are calm. Then treat the written version as the boss. You are not deciding in the moment. You are checking whether the moment matches the plan.

Common traps, in plain terms

Most mistakes come from a few habits of the mind. Knowing their names helps you spot them.

  • Loss aversion. A loss feels worse than an equal gain feels good. So you avoid closing a losing trade, because closing makes the loss real.
  • Selling winners early, holding losers. A gain feels fragile, so you grab it. A loss feels temporary, so you wait. The result is small wins and large losses, the opposite of a positive expectancy.
  • Overconfidence. A few good trades make you feel skilled, and you size up or skip the stop. Your next trade is no more certain than the last.
  • Fear of missing out. A stock runs without you, and you buy it after the move, with no entry plan and a distant stop.

None of these is a flaw to be ashamed of. They are the default. The rules exist to stand in their way.

The nerve to act

Acting means taking the stop when price reaches it, without negotiating. It means entering a valid setup even after a string of losses, because the setup does not know about your last trade. It also means not chasing: if price has left your entry behind, the trade is gone, and another will come.

Revenge trading deserves its own warning. After a loss, the urge to win it back at once leads to bigger size and weaker setups. The daily trade cap in your Investment Policy Statement is the guardrail. When you hit the cap, you are done for the day, however you feel.

You are not deciding in the moment. You are checking whether the moment matches the plan.

The nerve not to

Sometimes there is no setup. The correct trade is none. Sitting on your hands is an active choice, and it is often the hardest one, especially when you have time and a funded account in front of you. Active management depends on activity, but activity means the routine, not the number of orders.

The same restraint applies to the core. The core is built to be left alone. Tinkering with it because the satellite is exciting, or because the news is loud, defeats its purpose. Changes to the core follow the written policy, not your mood.

Measuring discipline

You cannot improve what you do not measure. The journal records, for every trade, whether you followed your own rules. That gives you a rule-adherence number. The Ladder's promotion rule asks for positive expectancy and at least 90% adherence. A profitable month with poor adherence does not count, because luck can look like skill for a while. Adherence is the evidence that the results came from the method.

Open questions

  • What exactly counts as a rule violation in the journal: a missed stop, an oversize position, an unplanned entry, all of them?
  • What is the daily trade cap, and what happens after two losses in a row?
  • Is there a cooling-off rule after a stop-out, and how long is it?
  • What are the written conditions under which the core may be changed at all?
  • How will you record the feeling behind a trade so patterns show up in the journal?

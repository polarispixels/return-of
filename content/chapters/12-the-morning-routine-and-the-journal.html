Active management depends on activity. The morning routine is the habit, and the journal is the evidence that you kept it.

Why a routine

A strategy that needs attention fails quietly when attention lapses. Stops go stale, setups pass unseen, and a position you meant to watch becomes one you forgot. The answer is not heroic effort. It is a short, fixed routine that happens at the same time each day.

The routine takes 30 minutes. Level 1 of the Ladder asks you to start it, along with the journal, before you place any active trade.

A simple 30-minute checklist

This is a generic starting point. Adjust it to your own rules.

  1. Review open positions and stops. For each position, check price against stop and target. Note anything that has changed.
  2. Check the watchlist for setups. Look for names that meet your written entry criteria. Skip anything that does not.
  3. Place or adjust orders. Enter planned trades with the stop attached, and adjust stops only in the direction of protection.
  4. Log it. Record the session and every trade in the journal before you close the screen.

If there is nothing to do, the routine is still done. A morning that ends in “no setups, no changes” counts, and the log says so.

What the journal records

The journal is a record of each trade, written at the time. Keep it plain and keep it complete.

What the journal computes

Two numbers matter most. The first is expectancy: the average result per trade, from your own wins and losses, as covered in chapter 10. The second is rule adherence: the share of trades where you followed your rules. Both come straight from the fields above.

These feed the Ladder. The promotion rule asks for positive expectancy and at least 90% adherence, shown in the journal. Level 4 adds a further gate: about 50 paper trades with positive expectancy. You move up on proof, not on feeling ready.

When the routine lapses

The plan also sets a default for neglect. If 10 trading days pass without the morning routine, the active slice goes back to the core. This is not a punishment. It is a safe default: money that nobody is watching returns to the part of the portfolio built to be left alone. Return of capital comes first.

Open questions

  • What time of day does the routine run, and what is the backup when you miss it?
  • Does a partial routine count toward the 10-day demotion rule, or only a full one?
  • Where does the journal live, and in what format, so it stays portable?
  • How are paper trades marked so they never mix with real ones in the expectancy figure?
  • How often do you review the journal as a whole, and what do you look for?

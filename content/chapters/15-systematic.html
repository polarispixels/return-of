Systematic means rules written once and run by software, and the only rules worth automating are the ones your journal has already proven.

Prove it by hand first

Code does what you tell it, every time, without doubt or fatigue. That is the strength and the danger. A bad rule in code is a bad rule executed faster and more often. So the order is fixed: run a rule by hand, record every trade in the journal, and let the evidence show whether the rule has positive expectancy and whether you follow it. Only then does the rule earn a place in code.

Strategy 13 on the menu has high effort up front and low effort after. Its risk depends entirely on the rules inside it. Software adds no edge of its own. It removes the friction of acting, and it removes some of your mistakes of mood.

The order of automation

Automate in three steps, and do not skip ahead.

  1. Record keeping. Pull trades into the journal by CSV export or broker API instead of typing them. Compute adherence and expectancy for you. This is the safest automation, because it cannot place an order.
  2. Alerts. Have software watch your price levels, stops and setups and notify you. You still decide and click. This keeps the morning routine short and removes the chore of staring at charts.
  3. Order entry. Let code place orders only for rules that passed the first two steps, and only with small size at first.

This is why the tool checklist in Chapter 13 asks about API access, charting integration and CSV export. You are choosing the tools that make these steps possible.

Backtesting and its traps

A backtest runs a rule over past prices to see what it would have done. It is useful for rejecting ideas. It is weak for confirming them. Three traps matter most.

  • Overfitting. Try enough settings and one will fit the past by luck. A rule tuned to fit history has learned the noise. Keep rules simple, with few settings, and test on data you did not use to build the rule.
  • Look-ahead. The test uses information that was not known at the moment of the trade, such as a closing price used to make a decision before the close. The result looks better than any real day could be.
  • Ignoring costs. Commissions, option contract fees, the gap between bid and ask, slippage and taxes all reduce the result. A rule that wins by a thin margin before costs can lose after them.

A backtest is a hypothesis, not proof. The journal, with real fills, is the proof.

Paper first, then small, with a kill switch

Run any new automated rule on paper trading before real money. Paper trading catches bugs, bad data and surprises in how orders behave. It does not show real fills or your own reactions, so move to real money small and scale only as the journal supports it.

Keep a human kill switch. You need one step, which you can take from your phone, that cancels open orders and stops the code. Set hard limits in the code itself: a maximum position size, a maximum number of orders per day (your trade cap), and a maximum loss that halts everything. Check on the system each morning as part of the routine. Software that nobody watches fails the principle that active management depends on activity.

A bad rule in code is a bad rule executed faster and more often.
